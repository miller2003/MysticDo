Methodology & Trust

Spiritual services carry a real trust problem. We don't ask you to trust us. We show our work — where facts come from, how the quiz thinks, what AI does and doesn't do, how money flows, and where this site is still unfinished.

At a glance

  • What we publish: decision guides, comparisons, an intent quiz, and free tools — not horoscopes, not an encyclopedia, not a link farm.
  • Provider facts come from the provider's own public pages, checked by us — never from reader-submitted bios or press releases.
  • Every claim has a class: verified by us, platform-stated, user-reported, or editorial judgment. The difference is labeled, not blurred.
  • Affiliate links pay for the site. They never decide rankings, and we say when free is enough — even when a paid option would earn us a commission.
  • AI is part of how this site is made. What it doesn't do: decide conclusions, verify facts, or publish. A human does all three.
  • Honest current state. Provider reviews are being written to the standard below and publish on a rolling basis; the quiz is rule-based. We'd rather say that plainly than let you discover it.

The five questions

Before the details, the short answers to the five questions a methodology page exists to answer.

Who

A small, independent project — not a company, an agency, or a content farm. Research and production are AI-assisted; a human reviews and approves everything before it publishes.

What

The decision layer of spiritual services: which kind of guidance fits which situation, what things cost, how providers differ, and what to do next.

How

Documented research: platform facts verified at the source, community signals read as patterns, structured editorial frameworks, and judgment that's labeled as judgment.

What we measure

Fit (situation to service), price mechanics, transparency, safety patterns, and whether a service helps you ask a better question — not whether it's "true."

What we don't claim

We don't claim any practice is real or fake, and nobody — including us — can guarantee a reading will help you. We help you decide what to do, not what to believe.

Our position on spiritual services

MysticDo does not attempt to prove that any spiritual practice is "real" or "true." That question is outside our scope — and outside what someone deciding whether to spend $40 on a reading actually needs answered by us.

What we do take a position on:

  • If you decide to try a service, we help you understand what it's for, who it suits, what it costs, and how to choose a provider.
  • We help you reduce unnecessary risk — financial, emotional, and the risk of being manipulated by an industry that sometimes preys on people at their lowest.
  • We say when free is enough, even when a paid option would earn us a commission.

Who is behind MysticDo

MysticDo is a small, independent project. We are not a company, a media group, or a marketing agency, and we don't write as if we were. Research and production are AI-assisted — and a human reviews every page before it publishes.

This is also not our first site in this space. MysticDo grew out of an earlier, smaller psychic review project where the core of this model — decision-first content, documented methods, disclosed affiliate links — was tested against real visitors and real (small) revenue. That project is where we learned how expensive honest research is, and how much of this industry's "information" is marketing in a costume.

Two things carried over from that project: the method, and the lessons. One thing deliberately did not: conclusions. MysticDo's provider coverage starts from zero. When we reference industry patterns, we label the evidence level (see below). We don't inherit old verdicts — that would be exactly the kind of stale, unearned authority this page exists to prevent.

How the work is divided

The pipeline is intentionally small and split into stages that cannot self-certify:

  1. Research stage — gathering provider pricing pages, policy documents, and community-signal threads; assembling comparison data; drafting decision frameworks. AI-assisted for scale and consistency.
  2. Editorial review stage — a human checks every material fact against its source, applies the evidence-level labels below, and decides what stays, what's downgraded, and what's cut. This stage cannot be skipped or automated.
  3. Publish stage — only after editorial review signs off does a page go live. Pricing snapshots are dated at this step; corrections are logged in the changelog below.

The roles here are functional, not titular. We don't list individuals, because the trust model rests on the process — verifiable sources, labeled evidence, dated updates — not on a personality. When a real, externally verifiable identity is ready to stand behind specific pages, we'll say so plainly. Until then, the method is the byline.

How the intent quiz works — and what it isn't

Do What Fits and the four category quizzes ask about your situation, not your birth data. Under the hood, the current version is a transparent rule-based matcher: your answers map to a small set of intent profiles (love, life direction, spiritual growth, loss), and each profile maps to the guidance formats that structurally fit that kind of question.

The quiz uses psychological relevance — questions that recognize your situation — to make guidance feel personal. This is a deliberate design choice, and it has a hard boundary: relevance is not diagnosis. When a result says "people in your situation often benefit from a reflective format," that's a pattern statement about many people, not a claim about you specifically. If you're in acute grief or crisis, the quiz points you to professional support before it points you to any service — because that's the one situation where a wrong recommendation costs more than money.

Its honest limits: it's rule-based, the question set is small, and it doesn't learn yet. Quiz results are a starting point for reading the right guide — never a verdict.

How a decision guide is built

Every commercial guide follows the same spine, because the reader's decision follows the same spine:

  1. Problem — name the actual situation you're in, in your language, not industry jargon.
  2. Context — what this kind of problem usually is, and what it usually isn't.
  3. Options — the realistic paths: psychic, tarot, astrology, medium, free tools, or nothing.
  4. Differences — what each path actually delivers: output format, personalization, time scale.
  5. Risks — the financial and emotional failure modes, including how this niche gets exploited.
  6. Decision criteria — the factors that should drive your choice, stated before any recommendation.
  7. Recommendation path — situations mapped to paths. Plural paths, because one size fits nobody.
  8. Action — the concrete next step, even when the next step is "don't spend money yet."

A guide that can't survive this structure usually isn't a decision guide — it's a sermon or a sales page. If you find one of ours that reads like either, that's a bug and we want to hear about it.

How comparisons stay honest

Comparison pages are where most sites quietly sell. Ours follow fixed rules:

  • Never a "best" verdict. Fit depends on your situation — that's the entire premise of this site.
  • Fixed dimensions: what question it fits, output format, personalization, time scale, typical price, who it suits, and limitations.
  • The limitations row is mandatory. A comparison without limitations is an advertisement with a table in it.
  • The decision stays with you. Our job is to make the differences visible, not to choose for you. See Psychic vs Tarot for the pattern in practice.

One rule disciplines all of it: we check the source rather than cite the middleman. When a price, policy, or feature matters to a decision, it gets verified on the provider's own platform before it enters a page. When we can't verify something, we say so — and the claim gets downgraded to the evidence level it deserves.

Evidence levels: what we know vs. what we think

Not all statements on this site carry the same weight. Every material claim belongs to one of four classes, and the wording on the page is built to show you which is which:

Level 1 — Verified by us

We checked it on the provider's platform ourselves: listed prices, policy pages, available formats, reader directory behavior.

Sounds like: "Chat rates start at $X per minute, as listed on reader profiles."

Level 2 — Platform-stated

The provider says so; we haven't stress-tested it. Useful, but it's their voice, not ours.

Sounds like: "The platform states it screens every applicant."

Level 3 — User-reported

Aggregated from reviews, forums, and community threads. Genuine signals; individually unverifiable.

Sounds like: "Refund complaints recur across independent review threads."

Level 4 — Editorial judgment

Our interpretation, framed as interpretation. It can be well-reasoned; it cannot be a fact.

Sounds like: "For your situation, a reflective format may fit better than a predictive one."

The rule that makes this useful: numbers come from Levels 1–2, and anything worded as "may fit" is Level 4. If you ever see a hard number presented as our judgment, or a judgment dressed up as a number, the page has failed its own standard.

Provider reviews: the standard we hold

Provider reviews are where trust matters most in this niche — so they're also where we're slowest. Every provider review published on MysticDo must complete a fixed template:

  1. Overview — what the service offers, factually and neutrally.
  2. Who it may suit — matched to question types, never "everyone."
  3. How the experience works — format, flow, and what actually happens.
  4. Pricing structure — with the date the prices were checked.
  5. Types of readers — what specializations actually exist.
  6. Strengths — what the service genuinely does well.
  7. Limitations — where it falls short. This section is mandatory; a review without it is an ad.
  8. Best use cases — which situations it fits.
  9. What to know before buying — the gotchas and the checks.
  10. How this review was researched — the evidence levels used, stated on the page.
  11. Alternative providers — because a review with no alternatives is a sales funnel.
  12. FAQ — the questions people actually ask.
  13. Next step — where to go from here, including "not this one."

Why a fixed template? To prevent the affiliate-review death spiral: five stars → five stars → "click here." A template forces the sections marketing would rather skip — limitations, alternatives, and the difference between what a platform claims and what users report.

  • When the first real reviews go live, each will state when prices were checked, what we verified versus what the platform claims, and which community signals we weighed.

Why you won't find overall scores here

You will not see a "9.4/10" next to any provider on MysticDo. This is deliberate, and it's the one place we diverge from most comparison sites by design:

  • A single score implies precision that doesn't exist for subjective services. "Accuracy" isn't measurable across readers, sessions, or the person asking.
  • Scores hide the dimension that matters to you. If you care most about price, a composite rating built mostly on other things will mislead you confidently.
  • Numbers invite verdict-shopping. We'd rather you comparison-think your situation than comparison-shop our conclusions.

Instead, provider pages carry structured dimensions — pricing mechanics, transparency, reader vetting, formats, red-flag patterns — and you do the weighting. This costs us the simplicity of a league table. We think it buys you something better.

Language rules: what we will and won't say

Trust is built or lost sentence by sentence. We keep two house lists, and every page is checked against them.

We write

  • "This type of reading is commonly used for…"
  • "People often choose this when…"
  • "If your main question is X, this format may fit better than Y."
  • "This may help you explore…"

We don't write

  • "This will tell you your future."
  • "Guaranteed accurate" — about any service, including our own guidance.
  • "The #1 psychic site of 2026."
  • Testimonial framing we can't verify — "she knew everything about me."

Two standing rules sit underneath both lists. First, we never promise outcomes — from any service, or from us. Second, we never write as if a practice's truth were settled, in either direction: devout belief and reflexive mockery are both positions you're entitled to hold, and neither is a job description for a decision guide.

How we use AI — exactly where, and where it stops

MysticDo is upfront about this: AI is part of how this site gets made. We use it for research assistance — clustering community threads, surfacing recurring patterns, drafting comparison rows — and for production drafting. That's the honest sentence most sites in this niche won't write.

Here is the rest of the sentence. What AI does not do at MysticDo:

  • Decide conclusions. Recommendations come from the frameworks on this page, applied by a person.
  • Verify facts. Every price, policy, and claim gets checked by a human against the source before it stays on a page.
  • Publish. Nothing goes live without human review — specifically on factual accuracy, affiliate compliance, provider claims, anything touching grief or crisis, and final sign-off.

If AI assists a page and we later find an error, that's our error, not the tool's. Accountability doesn't delegate.

Affiliate disclosure & editorial independence

Some links on MysticDo are affiliate links. If you sign up for a service through one of them, we may earn a commission at no additional cost to you. This is currently the site's primary revenue model — we're not going to describe a fantasy version of this site where money isn't involved.

The more interesting question is what the money cannot buy:

  • Ranking. Providers are not ranked by commission size. (We publish no overall rankings at all — see why.)
  • Inclusion. A provider is not recommended because it pays more. If it appears, it earned its place under the template.
  • Methodology. The same template, the same evidence levels, and the same limitations requirement apply to affiliate and non-affiliate providers alike.
  • "Free is enough" calls. When the right answer costs nothing, we say so — even when a paid affiliate option exists. You'll find this pattern throughout the guides.

Two further commitments. If a provider stops offering affiliate terms, it stays on the site if it remains a legitimate option for users. If a provider changes its terms in ways that harm users, it is removed — regardless of what it pays.

And one structural rule about placement: an affiliate link is the logical endpoint of a page, never its beginning. The sequence is problem → judgment → comparison → match → then the link. If you ever find a MysticDo page whose reasoning exists to justify a button, that page is broken — tell us and it gets fixed or pulled.

What we don't do

  • Claim any spiritual practice is real or fake.
  • Guarantee outcomes — theirs or ours.
  • Publish overall provider scores. Dimensions, not verdicts.
  • Sell rankings, placements, or links. (If that ever changes, sponsored content will be labeled in plain language, on the page, before you read it.)
  • Publish fabricated testimonials or invented user stories.
  • Present platform marketing as independent research.
  • Treat user reviews as proof of anything except what users report.
  • Recommend a paid service where free is genuinely enough.
  • Replace licensed medical, legal, financial, or therapeutic advice — see the disclaimers on every relevant page.

One note on what absence means: if a provider or reader isn't on this site, that's not a verdict. It usually means we haven't researched it yet. We don't publish hit pieces, and we don't run a blacklist — we'd rather teach the pattern than shout the name, which is why our safety content focuses on red flags you can check yourself.

Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires separating what will always limit this methodology from what merely limits it today.

Structural — these don't go away

  • We evaluate services and decision-fit, not the truth of spiritual claims. No methodology settles that question, and any site claiming otherwise is selling certainty it doesn't have.
  • Spiritual services are irreducibly subjective. A reader who gives one person genuine clarity will do nothing for the next. No framework, including ours, can predict your experience — only improve your odds of choosing well.
  • Community signals are noisy. Review platforms can be gamed, and forums over-represent extreme experiences. We weigh patterns, not anecdotes — and patterns can still be wrong.
  • Everything is a snapshot. Prices, policies, and reader rosters change. There is always lag between a change and our verification of it.

Current — on the record

  • Provider reviews are in progress. None are published yet; the standard they must meet is above.
  • The quiz is rule-based, not a trained model, and it doesn't learn from results yet.
  • Dating is real from September 2026 forward. Earlier drafts carried illustrative dates; every page now shows an actual revision date.

These limitations aren't legal hedging. They're the honest boundaries of what a small, independent operation can credibly claim — and respecting them is what separates a methodology from a marketing page.

Corrections

If you find a factual error — a wrong price, a dead link, a misattributed policy — tell us. The policy is simple:

  • Factual errors are corrected within 72 hours of verification, with the correction noted where space allows.
  • Methodology changes are never silent — they're documented in the changelog below with the date.
  • Disagreements with our judgment calls aren't errors; that's what the Level 4 label is for. You can discount our judgment with full information, which is more than most sites offer.
