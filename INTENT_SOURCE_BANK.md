## Love & relationships pool

### 1. Investment Model meta-analysis
- **Citation:** Tran, P., Judge, M., & Kashima, Y. (2019). Commitment in relationships: An updated meta-analysis of the Investment Model. *Personal Relationships, 26*(1), 158–180. doi:10.1111/pere.12268
- **Verified:** 2026-09-19 (University of Groningen portal + Wiley abstract).
- **Abstract supports:** 202 independent samples, 50,427 participants. Satisfaction had the largest aggregate association with commitment (r = 0.65), followed by investments (r = 0.53) and quality of alternatives (r = −0.43).
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2019 meta-analysis pooling 202 independent samples and 50,427 participants found that how much a person has invested in a relationship tracked their commitment to it (r = 0.53) — the second-strongest correlate after satisfaction itself (r = 0.65)."
- **Do NOT say:** that investment *causes* commitment, that the study proves anything about an individual, or that satisfaction is unimportant.
- **Used on:** does-he-love-me (2026-09-19).

### 2. Feelings vs. sustained caring behavior
- **Citation:** Kammrath, L. K., & Peetz, J. (2011). The limits of love: Predicting immediate versus sustained caring behaviors in close relationships. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 47*(2), 411–417. doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2010.11.004
- **Verified:** 2026-09-19 (OpenAIRE/PlumX/abstract).
- **Abstract supports:** Positive feelings predicted pro-relational behaviors when they could be completed immediately and in a short timespan, but NOT behaviors requiring days of delay or a week of sustained self-regulation. Sustained behavior was better predicted by self-regulation strength (trait conscientiousness) than by relationship feelings. "Immediate pro-relational behaviors are more likely to be acts of the heart, whereas delayed, sustained pro-relational behaviors are more likely to be acts of will."
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Warm feelings predicted the caring things people did immediately — but not the caring that required sustained effort over days and weeks; those were better predicted by self-regulation than by love."
- **Do NOT say:** feelings don't matter; that all consistent behavior is loveless; that conscientious people love less.
- **Used on:** does-he-love-me (2026-09-19); how-to-manifest-love (2026-09-29).

### 3. Romantic love as an attachment process

- **Abstract supports:** Romantic love can be conceptualized as an attachment process — a biosocial process by which affectional bonds form between adult lovers; adult styles (secure / anxious / avoidant) parallel infant patterns and shape how people experience love.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "The founding paper of adult attachment research described romantic love as an attachment process — a bond shaped by distinct styles (secure, anxious, avoidant) that change how people express and experience it."
- **Used on:** does-he-love-me (2026-09-19); how-to-manifest-love (2026-09-29); is-he-my-twin-flame (2026-09-29).

### 7. Deception-detection accuracy meta-analysis
- **Citation:** Bond, C. F., Jr., & DePaulo, B. M. (2006). Accuracy of deception judgments. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 10*(3), 214–234. doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1003_2
- **Verified:** 2026-09-21 (PubMed PMID 16859438 + Google Scholar record).
- **Abstract supports:** 206 documents, 24,483 judges. People achieve an average of 54% correct lie–truth judgments, correctly classifying 47% of lies as deceptive and 61% of truths as nondeceptive. Experts were not significantly better than nonexperts; people tend to regard their interaction partners as honest.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2006 meta-analysis pooling 206 studies and 24,483 judges found people distinguish lies from truths at about 54% accuracy — barely above chance — and that expertise doesn't help."

### 2. The planning fallacy
- **Citation:** Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67*(3), 366–381.
- **Verified:** 2026-09-21 (MIT-hosted full PDF; replicated commentary).
- **Abstract supports:** People underestimate their own but not others' completion times, focus on plan-based scenarios rather than past experience, and attribute past failures to external, transient, specific factors. In the thesis study, the average best guess was 33.9 days, the actual completion 55.5 days, and only about 30% finished by their predicted date; even the pessimistic estimate came in short.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "The classic demonstration of the planning fallacy found students predicted 33.9 days to finish a thesis and took 55.5 — with only about 30% finishing by their predicted date."
- **Do NOT say:** that every estimate is late, or that hiring timelines can be predicted from it.
- **Used on:** will-i-get-the-job (2026-09-21).

### 3. Affective forecasting / impact bias
- **Citation:** Wilson, T. D., & Gilbert, D. T. (2005). Affective forecasting: Knowing what to want. *Current Directions in Psychological Science, 14*(3), 131–135. doi:10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x
- **Verified:** 2026-09-21 (CAPES record + author's Harvard PDF).
- **Abstract supports:** People base decisions on affective forecasts — predictions of their emotional reactions to future events — and display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of those reactions. One cause is focalism.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Affective-forecasting research finds people overestimate the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to future outcomes — an effect called the impact bias."
- **Do NOT say:** that the outcome doesn't matter, or that the bias erases real disappointment.
- **Used on:** will-i-get-the-job (2026-09-21).

### 1. Habit formation timelines in the real world
- **Citation:** Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology, 40*(6), 998–1009. doi:10.1002/ejsp.674
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (UCL record + multiple secondary summaries of the study).
- **Abstract supports:** 96 volunteers repeated a self-chosen daily behavior in a consistent context for 12 weeks; median time to reach 95% of the automaticity plateau was 66 days, with individual range 18–254 days. Missing a single opportunity did not measurably affect the formation curve; behavior complexity drove the variation.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2010 UCL study tracked 96 people forming a daily habit and found the median time to automaticity was 66 days, with individuals ranging from 18 to 254 — and missing a single day didn't measurably set the process back."
- **Do NOT say:** that "21 days" is scientific (it traces to a surgeon's 1960 observation about surgical adjustment, not habit research); that 66 days applies to everyone; that the study was about manifestation.
- **Used on:** 369-manifestation-method (2026-09-29); scripting-manifestation (2026-09-29).

### 2. Positive fantasies vs. positive expectations
- **Citation:** Oettingen, G., & Mayer, D. (2002). The motivating function of thinking about the future: Expectations versus fantasies. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 83*(5), 1198–1212. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1198
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (APA PsycInfo record via Scholar + NYU-hosted full text).
- **Abstract supports:** Across four longitudinal domains (graduates seeking jobs, students with a crush, undergraduates facing exams, hip-replacement patients), positive expectations (judging the desired future as likely) predicted higher effort and better outcomes, while positive fantasies (experiencing the desired future as already good) predicted the opposite — measured weeks to two years later.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2002 study that followed graduates, students, and patients across four domains found positive expectations predicted higher effort and better outcomes — while positive fantasies, the kind that experience the desired future as already here, predicted the reverse."
- **Do NOT say:** that positive thinking is always harmful; that visualization has no value; that fantasy directly causes failure.
- **Used on:** 369-manifestation-method (2026-09-29); scripting-manifestation (2026-09-29); how-to-manifest-love (2026-09-30); how-to-manifest-a-specific-person (2026-09-30).

### 3. The self-fulfilling prophecy — the founding statement
- **Citation:** Merton, R. K. (1948). The self-fulfilling prophecy. *The Antioch Review, 8*(2), 193–210. doi:10.2307/4609267
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (multiple independent scholarly citations incl. Springer + Sage reference lists; JSTOR record).
- **Abstract supports:** Merton's founding definition: a false definition of the situation evoking a new behavior which makes the originally false conception come "true." The paper's examples range from bank runs (the Last National Bank of fictional Cartwrightville) to racial and labor discrimination — belief about a situation changes behavior, and the changed behavior makes the belief come true.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Sociologist Robert K. Merton named the mechanism in 1948: a false definition of a situation evokes behavior that makes the originally false conception come true — the belief changes the behavior, and the behavior changes the outcome."
- **Do NOT say:** that the mechanism validates manifestation metaphysics (Merton's prophecies run through observable behavior, not consciousness rearranging events); that every belief self-fulfills.
- **Used on:** law-of-assumption (2026-09-29).

### 4. Teacher expectancy and IQ gains (Pygmalion)
- **Citation:** Rosenthal, R., & Jacobson, L. (1966). Teachers' expectancies: Determinants of pupils' IQ gains. *Psychological Reports, 19*(1), 115–118. doi:10.2466/pr0.1966.19.1.115
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (SAGE publisher record + full-text PDF; PubMed PMID 5942071).
- **Abstract supports:** Within each of 18 classrooms, an average of 20% of children were reported to teachers as showing unusual potential for intellectual gains. Eight months later these children — actually selected at random — showed significantly greater IQ gains than controls. Effects operated primarily among younger children. Note: later replication attempts have been mixed.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "In a 1966 field experiment, teachers were told certain randomly chosen children were about to bloom intellectually; eight months later those children had gained more IQ than classmates — expectations had shifted the ordinary interactions that produce a change. Later replications have been mixed, and the studied effect is modest and interpersonal, not a law of the universe."
- **Do NOT say:** that the study proves assumptions directly alter external reality (the mechanism runs through behavior — the teachers', not the children's); that the effect is large, universal, or uncontested.
- **Used on:** law-of-assumption (2026-09-29).

### 5. Mental imagery evokes more emotion than verbal representation
- **Citation:** Holmes, E. A., & Mathews, A. (2010). Mental imagery in emotion and emotional disorders. *Clinical Psychology Review, 30*(3), 349–362. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2010.01.001 (PMID 20116915)
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (Oxford Research Archive record + PubMed record; volume/issue/pages/DOI cross-checked against PMC reference lists).
- **Abstract supports:** The review confirms that mental imagery evokes greater emotional responses than verbal representation, and that the extent of the emotional response depends on the image perspective adopted; imagery connects to emotional systems directly, overlaps perception ("as if" responding), and contacts autobiographical emotional memories.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2010 review in Clinical Psychology Review confirmed that mental imagery evokes stronger emotional responses than verbal representation — and that the size of the response depends on the perspective the image is taken from."
- **Do NOT say:** that the review studied manifestation or scripting (it studied emotion and emotional disorders — it supports the imagery-emotion mechanism, not the metaphysics); that vividness alone produces outcomes.

### 6. Acute aerobic exercise raises positive activated affect
- **Citation:** Reed, J., & Ones, D. S. (2006). The effect of acute aerobic exercise on positive activated affect: A meta-analysis. *Psychology of Sport and Exercise, 7*(5), 477–514. doi:10.1016/j.psychsport.2005.11.003
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (University of Minnesota research portal record + ScienceDirect publisher record; volume/issue/pages/DOI cross-checked).
- **Abstract supports:** Meta-analysis of 158 studies (1979–2005) yielding 450 independent effect sizes and 13,101 participants; overall mean corrected effect size on self-reported positive activated affect was .47 (SD .37). Effects were consistently positive immediately post-exercise, when pre-exercise affect was lower than average, for low intensity (<15–39% VO2R), for durations up to ~35 min, and for low-to-moderate doses; effects lasted at least 30 min. Control conditions were associated with reductions in affect (mean −.17).
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2006 meta-analysis of 158 studies found acute aerobic exercise reliably raised self-reported positive activated affect (a mean effect around 0.47), with the largest gains when the pre-exercise state was lower than usual — and control conditions lowered affect."
- **Do NOT say:** that the meta-analysis tested "vibration" or attraction (it measured self-reported mood); that exercise substitutes for treatment of depression or anxiety; that the effect is uniform (large SDs suggest individual differences moderate it).
- **Used on:** how-to-raise-your-vibration (2026-09-29).

### 7. Gratitude listing and subjective well-being
- **Citation:** Emmons, R. A., & McCullough, M. E. (2003). Counting blessings versus burdens: An experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84*(2), 377–389. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377 (PMID 12585811)
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (PubMed record + APA PsycNet record; volume/issue/pages/DOI cross-checked).
- **Abstract supports:** Across three studies, participants were randomized to gratitude-listing, hassle-listing, or neutral/comparison conditions (daily or weekly records); the gratitude groups showed heightened well-being across several — though not all — outcome measures, with the effect on positive affect the most robust finding. Study 3 used persons with neuromuscular disease.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2003 randomized study assigned participants to gratitude listing, hassle listing, or neutral conditions; the gratitude groups showed heightened well-being across several measures, with the effect on positive affect the most robust result."
- **Do NOT say:** that the study measured vibration or attraction (it measured subjective well-being); that forced or stated gratitude works as well as felt gratitude (the qualifier matters); that the findings are universal across all outcomes (the paper itself notes several measures showed no difference).
- **Used on:** how-to-raise-your-vibration (2026-09-29).

## Loss & closure pool

### 1. Continuing bonds — the grief framework
- **Citation:** Klass, D., Silverman, P. R., & Nickman, S. L. (Eds.). (1996). *Continuing bonds: New understandings of grief.* Philadelphia: Taylor & Francis.
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (book; publisher record via multiple library/academic citations; no DOI — same convention as the Grof entry below).
- **Abstract supports:** The edited volume that introduced the continuing-bonds framework: maintaining a connection with the deceased — through memory, ritual, and internalized presence — is a normal and often healthy dimension of grieving, in contrast with older models that demanded detachment as the endpoint of mourning.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "The continuing-bonds framework, introduced in a 1996 edited volume by Klass, Silverman, and Nickman, reframed ongoing connection with someone who died as a normal, often healthy part of grief rather than a failure to let go."
- **Do NOT say:** that the framework certifies specific experiences (a bird visit, a dream) as messages from the deceased; that ongoing connection replaces clinical care where functioning declines; that it is uncontested — it revised the detachment model, and the field continues to debate its boundaries.
- **Used on:** cardinal-meaning (2026-09-29).

### 2. Sensory experiences of the deceased in bereavement — the integrative review
- **Citation:** Kamp, K. S., Steffen, E. M., Alderson-Day, B., Allen, P., Austad, A., Hayes, J., Larøi, F., Ratcliffe, M., & Sabucedo, P. (2020). Sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased in bereavement: An interdisciplinary and integrative review. *Schizophrenia Bulletin, 46*(6), 1367–1381. doi:10.1093/schbul/sbaa113
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (Schizophrenia Bulletin article page + PubMed + cited in Sage/PMC presence-experience literature).
- **Abstract supports:** Interdisciplinary integrative review of sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased (felt presence, auditory, visual, olfactory, tactile). These experiences are common across cultures (reported by 30–60% of bereaved individuals), predominantly experienced as comforting, and should not automatically be viewed as pathological — they function as adaptive responses that help maintain emotional bonds, consistent with the continuing-bonds framework.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2020 interdisciplinary review found that sensing a deceased loved one's presence — through felt presence, sound, sight, or scent — is reported by 30 to 60 percent of bereaved people across cultures, and is predominantly experienced as comforting rather than pathological."
- **Do NOT say:** that these experiences prove after-death contact; that they are always benign (5–30% are distressing); that the review covers bird sightings specifically — it covers felt/sensory presence, which the cardinal page extends by analogy.
- **Used on:** cardinal-meaning (2026-09-30).

### 1. Lacking control increases illusory pattern perception
- **Citation:** Whitson, J. A., & Galinsky, A. D. (2008). Lacking control increases illusory pattern perception. *Science, 322*(5898), 115–117. doi:10.1126/science.1159845

### 3. The continuity hypothesis of dream content
- **Citation:** Schredl, M., & Hofmann, F. (2003). Continuity between waking activities and dream activities. *Consciousness and Cognition, 12*(2), 298–308. doi:10.1016/S1053-8100(02)00072-7
- **Verified:** 2026-09-29 (PubMed PMID 12763010 + Google Scholar record, 526 citations).
- **Abstract supports:** Dream content reflects waking-life activities — interindividual differences in time spent on specific waking activities show up in dream content; the incorporation rate is modulated by factors including type of experience and emotional involvement. The authors caution the hypothesis in its general form needs further specification.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Dream-diary research on the continuity hypothesis finds that dream content tracks waking life — who and what occupies your days (and your emotional involvement with them) is what shows up in your dreams. A person appearing in your dream is evidence they are active in *your* waking emotional life; it is not evidence of anything about them."
- **Do NOT say:** that the continuity hypothesis is settled law in its strongest form (the authors themselves call for specification), or that it disproves non-ordinary dream experiences — it bounds what dream content can be *evidence of*.

### 4. Ultraweak photon emission from the human body (biophotons)

### 5. Just-world research
- **Citation:** Lerner, M. J., & Miller, D. T. (1978). Just world research and the attribution process: Looking back and ahead. *Psychological Bulletin, 85*(5), 1030–1051. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.85.5.1030

- **Abstract supports:** Review of the just-world hypothesis literature: people have a need to believe their environment is just and orderly — that people get what they deserve — and this need shapes reactions to innocent suffering and attributions about others' fates. - **Safe page phrasing:** "The just-world line of research describes a need to believe that people get what they deserve — a need that quietly shapes how fates, misfortunes, and debts get explained."

## Shadow / integration pool

_Pool note: "the shadow" is a depth-psychology construct (Jung), not a measurable quantity. Sources here support adjacent, testable claims — defensive projection and self-criticism outcomes — never "the shadow" itself. The page's caveat line must say so explicitly._

### 1. Defensive projection — suppression makes disowned traits accessible
- **Citation:** Newman, L. S., Duff, K. J., & Baumeister, R. F. (1997). A new look at defensive projection: Thought suppression, accessibility, and biased person perception. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 72*(5), 980–1001. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.72.5.980
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (PubMed PMID 9150580 + Ovid full-text record + Baumeister defense-mechanisms review PDF).
- **Abstract supports:** People often perceive in others qualities they wish to deny in themselves, but classical Freudian projection had limited empirical support. Newman et al. propose that suppressing thoughts about an unwanted trait makes that trait category chronically accessible, so it is then used to interpret others' behavior. Studies 1–4 showed repressors (who both avoid thinking about threatening traits and deny having them) readily infer those traits from others; Studies 5–6 experimentally assigned unfavorable traits to participants who, when asked or predisposed not to think about them, subsequently projected them onto someone else. Verdict: projection is better explained by thought suppression and accessibility than by the Freudian defense.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 1997 line of experiments found that trying to suppress a distasteful trait about yourself makes that trait more cognitively available — so you end up reading it into other people. The mechanism is suppression and accessibility, not a mystical exchange."
- **Do NOT say:** that this proves the Jungian shadow; that projection is always unconscious or always a defense (the modern model reframes it); that the studies measured 'shadow material' — they measured accessibility and person perception in lab settings.
- **Used on:** how-to-do-shadow-work (2026-09-30).

### 2. Self-criticism and psychotherapy outcome — the "not shame" anchor
- **Citation:** Löw, C. A., Schauenburg, H., & Dinger, U. (2020). Self-criticism and psychotherapy outcome: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Clinical Psychology Review, 75*, 101808. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2019.101808
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (ScienceDirect record S0272735819303204 + PubMed PMID 31864153 + Google Scholar/Mendeley citation records).
- **Abstract supports:** First meta-analysis of self-criticism and psychotherapy outcome: 49 longitudinal studies (56 independent effect sizes; 3,277 patients). The overall association between pre-treatment self-criticism and outcome was r = −.20 (95% CI −.25 to −.16, p < .0001) — higher self-criticism related to poorer outcome. The association varied by disorder (stronger for eating disorders). Findings support the relevance of self-criticism for psychotherapy outcome.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "A 2020 meta-analysis of 49 studies found that heavier self-criticism predicts a poorer therapy outcome — which is why the honest instruction in shadow work is retrieval, not shame."
- **Do NOT say:** that this is a trial of shadow work; that it shows self-compassion cures anything; that the effect is large — r ≈ −.20 is small-to-moderate; that it measures 'integration' or any depth-psychology claim.
- **Used on:** how-to-do-shadow-work (2026-09-30).

## Past lives & reincarnation pool

_Pool note: this is a contested domain. Stevenson's cases are the most rigorous evidence *for* the reincarnation hypothesis, and they are also the most-criticized; the skeptics' alternative (source-monitoring failure / cryptomnesia) is well documented but does not claim to explain every case. Pages must present both, attribute the verification to the original investigators, and never assert a solved answer either way._

### 1. The children-cases corpus — the strongest case *for*
- **Citation:** Stevenson, I. (1974). *Twenty cases suggestive of reincarnation* (2nd rev. ed.). Charlottesville, VA: University Press of Virginia. [Originally published 1966 as *Proceedings of the American Society for Psychical Research*, vol. 26.]
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (WorldCat record OCLC 1067306 + University of Southern Indiana library catalogue + Google Books bibliographic record; 2nd ed., xvi + 396 pp., ISBN 9780813905464).
- **Abstract supports:** Detailed case reports of twenty children (from India, Ceylon/Sri Lanka, Brazil, the Tlingit of Alaska, and Lebanon) who claimed to remember a previous life; Stevenson — a University of Virginia professor of psychiatry — presents interviews and follow-up investigations, including cases where statements were said to be verified and where birthmarks/birth defects corresponded to the remembered deceased person. The book presents the cases and discusses results of follow-up interviews; it does not claim a mechanism, and does not claim the totality of cases is verified.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "The most rigorous case *for* the phenomenon comes from Ian Stevenson's decades at the University of Virginia: he documented children — mostly aged two to five — who reported detailed apparent memories of previous lives, some of which were later said to be verified. His method has been heavily criticised and also replicated; he was not a credulous investigator."
- **Do NOT say:** that reincarnation is established; that the cases are unexamined or that all were verified; that Stevenson had no critics — his work is the most contested in the field, and the page must say so.
- **Used on:** past-life-regression (2026-09-30).

### 2. Cryptomnesia — the source-monitoring anchor
- **Citation:** Brown, A. S., & Murphy, D. R. (1989). Cryptomnesia: Delineating inadvertent plagiarism. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 15*(3), 432–442. doi:10.1037/0278-7393.15.3.432
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (APA *Monitor* feature on the study + Loterre/CogMemo thesaurus entry with full DOI + the article PDF hosted at warwick.ac.uk).
- **Abstract supports:** Three laboratory experiments: subjects generating category exemplars in turn with others inadvertently plagiarised others' responses in all three tasks (generation, recall-own, recall-new) despite instructions to avoid it; plagiarism was greater under more complex generation sequences, was most likely sourced from the person who responded just before the subject, and occurred for orthographic more than semantic categories. Cryptomnesia = a memory that is objectively present but not subjectively recognised as such — a source-monitoring failure where content is retained but the origin ("who said it, when") is lost.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Cryptomnesia is the documented phenomenon where a memory is objectively present but not recognised as such — experimenters have induced inadvertent plagiarism in 3–9% of cases in the lab. It is a real, ordinary cognitive failure: the content is retained, the source is lost."
- **Do NOT say:** that cryptomnesia explains *every* regression case — it is a partial explanation, and the paper itself describes it as a source-monitoring failure for *easily remembered* material (ideas, jokes, melodies, short phrases), which is precisely why it cannot account for elaborate narrative detail.
- **Used on:** past-life-regression (2026-09-30).

### 1. The text-history anchor — where the modern system actually comes from

### 3. Interoceptive accuracy vs awareness — the three-factor model
- **Citation:** Garfinkel, S. N., Seth, A. K., Barrett, A. B., Suzuki, K., & Critchley, H. D. (2015). Knowing your own heart: Distinguishing interoceptive accuracy from interoceptive awareness. *Biological Psychology, 104*, 65–74. doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2014.11.004
- **Verified:** 2026-09-30 (multiple independent citation records: PMC review article, University of Luxembourg ORBilu bibliography, Springer chapter reference list, and the DOI resolver — all giving volume 104, pp. 65–74, same DOI).
- **Abstract supports:** Proposes a three-factor model of interoception: interoceptive *accuracy* (objective performance on a heartbeat-detection task), interoceptive *sensibility* (self-reported belief about one's interoceptive ability), and interoceptive *awareness* (the correspondence between accuracy and sensibility, i.e. metacognitive insight). The study demonstrates these are dissociable — people can be accurate without knowing it, or believe themselves sensitive while performing poorly.
- **Safe page phrasing:** "Garfinkel and colleagues (2015) showed that how well you actually detect internal bodily signals (accuracy) and how well you know how good you are at it (awareness) are genuinely separate capacities — which is a useful fact when you are trying to read where you hold tension: 'I can't tell' is a real and common answer."
- **Do NOT say:** that this measures chakra function or "blocked" energy; that higher interoceptive accuracy is always better (the paper is careful that the factors dissociate, and high sensibility with low accuracy is not adaptive); that this validates any energy-healing claim; that it was conducted on meditators or spiritual practitioners (it was not).
- **Used on:** which-chakra-is-blocked (2026-09-30).
