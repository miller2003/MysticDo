What Is Karma?

“Karma” may be the most widely misused spiritual concept in Western popular culture: “karma will get him” is not karma, “bad karma” as a status is not karma, and “good karma” as good vibes is not karma. The actual teaching — more rigorous, more demanding, more useful than the punitive cosmic-justice story — is below: what it says in its original Hindu and Buddhist contexts, the three types, what the popular version gets wrong at each level, and what the teaching genuinely asks of you.

Direct answer

Karma is not a reward-and-punishment system administered by the universe. In its original Hindu and Buddhist formulations it is the principle that actions have consequences — that what you do, think, and intend creates conditions, and those conditions shape future experience, with the most important consequence being the effect on your own consciousness. That makes it a first-person instruction, not a spectator sport: the tradition’s own questions are what are you doing, intending, creating — not when will they get theirs. Key takeaways

  • Karma means action, not retribution. The Sanskrit word is “deed” — the teaching is that action creates conditions, not that the universe administers justice on a schedule.
  • The most important consequence is internal. In both Hindu and Buddhist framings, the primary effect of an action is on the actor’s own consciousness — greed practiced becomes greed reinforced. The mechanism is as much psychological as cosmic.
  • Intention is primary in the Buddhist formulation. The volition behind an action creates the karmic result — which makes the quality of mind the real work, not the visible deed.
  • The popular version makes you a spectator; the teaching makes you a practitioner. “Karma will get him” positions you as neutral observer of someone else’s account. The original teaching asks what conditions you are creating — harder, and the only domain you actually influence.
  • Nobody can clear your karma for a fee. In the traditions themselves, karma is worked with through practice and changed action. Anyone selling karmic clearance has left the teaching — and monetized the guilt it never issued.

“What is karma?” is not one question

The question sounds like a request for a definition. In practice it blends several, and which one is live changes what an honest answer looks like — because one of them is answerable from the texts, one is a hurt looking for an instrument, and one is a market looking for a customer.

The reframe that changes everything: karma is not primarily a belief to hold but an instruction to follow — and the instruction is first-person. Most dissatisfaction with the concept comes from holding it as a belief about other people’s accounts, which is the one use the teaching never supports.

Why the popular version of karma fails here

The “karma will get him” version misreads the teaching at four levels, each worth naming because each has a cost:

  • It externalizes the mechanism. Popular karma has the universe as judge and bailiff. The original teaching operates through the internal logic of cause and effect — actions create conditions, conditions shape experience — with the consciousness of the actor as the primary site. Nothing administers it from outside; it accumulates like compound interest on the mind.
  • It makes karma purely punitive. Popular karma is a punishment system. The original is a consequence system — positive, neutral, and difficult consequences alike — and the most important consequence in both traditions is the effect on one’s own consciousness, not on external circumstances.
  • It assumes visible, timely manifestation. “He’ll get his” requires consequence to arrive on camera. In the teaching, karmic consequences can extend across lifetimes and manifest unrecognizably — which makes karma a genuinely terrible tool for tracking other people’s moral accounts, the very use the popular version puts it to.
  • It makes the speaker a spectator. This is the deepest cost: “karma will get him” positions you as a neutral observer waiting for justice — a seat the teaching never offers. The actual instruction is first-person: what are you doing, intending, creating? Harder. And the only seat with any agency in it.

The alternative isn’t abandoning the concept. It’s recovering the teaching underneath the vocabulary — which turns out to be more demanding, and more useful, in direct proportion to how much of the popular version you let go.

Karma in its original context: the Hindu teaching

The Sanskrit karma means “action” or “deed.” In the Hindu philosophical tradition — particularly the Bhagavad Gita and the Upanishads — karma is the mechanism by which action creates consequence: every action of body, speech, or mind plants a seed, and seeds ripen. The element the popular version drops: the ripening happens in the actor’s own consciousness as much as in the world. Act from greed and you haven’t merely done a greedy thing — you’ve reinforced the mental formation of greed, making its recurrence more likely. The consequence is in the mind before it is in the circumstances.

This is why the Gita’s most famous instruction on karma is not about punishment but about the quality of action: “You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your action.” (2.47). Act from dharma — right action — and release attachment to specific outcomes. Karma is the principle that shapes what happens; dharma is the principle that guides what to do. The popular version collapses both into a ledger of deserts, which is the one reading the text does not support.

Karma in Buddhism: the intention-first formulation

Buddhist teaching is related but distinct, and its distinctiveness matters: in the Buddhist framework, karma is intention — the volition behind an action is what creates the karmic result, not the visible deed. An accidentally harmful action creates less karma than a deliberately harmful one; truth spoken with care and truth spoken cruelly are different karmic acts. The mental state is primary.

Buddhism also emphasizes karma operating within this lifetime — the immediate, ongoing effect of mental states on the quality of experience. The angry mind creates an angry experience; the generous mind, a generous one; the compassionate mind is itself described as a form of liberation. The mechanism here is not supernatural: it is the ordinary causality of how minds work — which makes the teaching verifiable in the only laboratory any of us actually occupies. The extension to past and future lives adds a further layer — accumulated karma shaping the conditions of each birth — and this is where the framework meets the Western vocabulary of soul lessons and karmic relationships: see Am I in a Karmic Relationship? for where that overlap helps and where it goes wrong.

The three types of karma — and the question they answer

Hindu philosophy divides karma three ways, and the structure answers the objection the popular version can’t:

  • Sanchita — the accumulated stock: everything created across lifetimes not yet ripened. The full account.
  • Prarabdha — the portion allotted to this lifetime: the conditions of your birth, your initial circumstances, your characteristic tendencies. Sometimes described as fate; more accurately, starting conditions.
  • Kriyamana (Agami) — the karma being created now, through present action. The domain of active choice — what you do with the conditions you’ve been given — and the primary site of human agency in the framework.

The structure matters because it answers the question that sinks the naive version: if karma explains everything, why do innocent people suffer? The framework’s answer — prarabdha may reflect prior actions the current self has no conscious access to — doesn’t make the suffering acceptable or the harm-doers unaccountable (their kriyamana is being created in real time). But it dissolves the arithmetic the popular version depends on: the universe is not running a this-life reward schedule that victims failed. It was never that kind of ledger.

What research can — and can’t — tell you

Whether karmic consequences extend across lifetimes is not a question research can examine — no instrument reaches it, and honest engagement means saying so. What psychology can do is explain why the popular version is so persistent: classic research on belief in a just world documents the deep human tendency to see outcomes as deserved — good things for good people, suffering as somehow earned. The punitive karma of popular culture is that reflex wearing Sanskrit: it feels true because meaning-making around suffering is a documented human default, not because anyone audited an account. A 2019 cross-cultural study in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin examined belief in karma directly — not as a proxy for just-world thinking, but as its own construct — and found that its content and its correlates vary across cultures, which complicates any single reading of what “karma” is doing psychologically.

What the just-world finding cannot do is settle the metaphysics — it explains the appeal of a story, not its truth or falsity. What it can do is sharpen the practical question: since the reflex will generate “they deserved it” readings automatically, the teaching’s insistence on first-person attention looks less like dogma and more like a correction for a documented bias. The tradition, on this point, anticipated the laboratory — the instruction to attend to your own actions and intentions is precisely the discipline that keeps the just-world reflex from running your interpretation of other people’s lives.

  • White, C. J. M., Norenzayan, A., & Schaller, M. (2019). The content and correlates of belief in Karma across cultures. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 45(9), 1184–1201.

What karma actually asks of you

Take the original teaching seriously rather than the popular version, and it makes three demands — none of which involves watching anyone else’s account:

  • Attend to your own actions. Not to other people’s karmic ledgers. Your actions create your conditions — that is the one domain with your fingerprints on it, and the only one the teaching assigns you.
  • Attend to intention as much as action. In the Buddhist formulation especially: the quality of mind behind the act is the primary determinant. Truth spoken harshly and truth spoken with care are different acts. Generosity from ego and generosity from care are different seeds. This makes the internal life the actual workplace — more demanding than any ritual, and available at no cost.
  • Release attachment to fruit. The Gita’s most radical instruction: act from what is genuinely right, and release the claim on specific outcomes. Not passivity — the recognition that your karma lives in the action and intention, while the fruit was never yours to control. Practically, this is the difference between doing good and doing good expecting returns, and the traditions are unanimous that the second is its own kind of seed.

And what it asks of you regarding others: nothing that looks like keeping score. The teaching’s silence on other people’s accounts isn’t an oversight — it’s the design. A framework that filled its practitioners with vigilance about others’ deserts would be manufacturing exactly the mental states (judgment, grievance, resentment) that the teaching identifies as the seeds of future suffering. The spectator’s seat isn’t just useless; it’s counterproductive on the teaching’s own terms.

Karma and spiritual reading

In the karma-attentive traditions, astrology reads the chart as a map of the conditions this lifetime is organized around — the South Node as accumulated material from prior development, Saturn as the specific tests this life emphasizes, Pluto as where deep transformation is required. Used honestly, this is territory-description, not fate-announcement: the reading describes the board, not the moves. A psychic reading oriented around karma is most useful when framed as “what pattern is at work here, and what is it asking of me?” — and least useful, per everything above, when framed as “is karma going to punish this person?” The second question is outside what any reading can address; the first is squarely within what a thoughtful one can engage.

If you still want a perspective beyond the teaching

Sometimes you’ve got the teaching straight — and something in you still wants an outside read on the pattern your own life keeps producing. That is a legitimate want, and the karma-attentive traditions have a specific, honest version of it: an astrological reading of the chart as territory — the nodes and Saturn as the map of conditions, not a verdict — or a reflective spread on what a repeating situation is asking of you. What no honest reading does is adjudicate anyone’s karmic account, pronounce on their deserts, or sell you a clearing. The next section is the checklist for the seller who does.

What you may actually be trying to know

“What is karma?” usually resolves into one of five more specific questions — and which one it is changes what actually helps. The pattern check above can surface this for your situation; the currents below are the general map.

1. “What does the teaching actually say?”

The understanding current — the page’s home ground. What fits: the original contexts above, and primary-text patience over summary folklore. The Hindu and Buddhist teachings differ enough that conflating them produces most of the popular confusion.

2. “Will they face consequences for what they did?”

The justice current — usually carrying a real injury. What fits: the honest redirect. The teaching gives you no window into anyone’s account, and the vigilance costs you the attention it claims to seek justice with. Accountability belongs to courts, communities, and boundaries — real instruments, unlike the cosmic waiting room. If the injury is deep, that’s therapist territory, and the redirect is not a dismissal of it.

3. “Why does the same thing keep happening to me?”

The pattern current — where the framework has its most practical use. What fits: pattern-oriented work — natal astrology as territory-map, reflective tarot, or therapy for the loop itself. The karmic frame adds one useful question: what is this situation asking of me? — and deliberately refuses its popular cousin: whose fault is it?

4. “Can my karma be cleared?”

The clearance current — usually arriving with a price attached. What fits: the traditions’ own answer — karma is worked with through practice and changed action; nobody holds your account; nothing is cleared by fee. What the honest version offers instead of a clearing: the first-person work this page describes, which is free and permanent.

5. “Is this happening because of a past life?”

The inheritance current. What fits: the prarabdha frame — starting conditions, not sentence — and the honesty that no one can verify the past-life story. The framework’s practical yield doesn’t depend on the metaphysics: whether the conditions came from before this life or from its first decade, the working domain is the same — what you do now. Common questions

Karma — honestly asked

What does karma actually mean?

The Sanskrit word means “action” or “deed.” In its original Hindu and Buddhist contexts, karma is the principle that actions have consequences — that actions of body, speech, and mind create conditions, and those conditions shape future experience. Crucially, the most important consequence in both traditions is internal: the effect of the action on the actor’s own consciousness. Greed practiced becomes greed reinforced. It is a first-person teaching about how action shapes the mind that acts — not a cosmic court administering punishments on schedule, which is the version popular culture substituted and the traditions never taught.

What is the difference between karma in Hinduism and Buddhism?

Two related teachings with different emphases. Hindu philosophy frames karma as the mechanism by which all action seeds and ripens across lifetimes, with the Bhagavad Gita’s central instruction being to act from dharma while releasing attachment to outcomes. Buddhism makes intention primary: the volition behind the act is what creates the karmic result, and the tradition emphasizes karma’s operation within this lifetime — the immediate effect of mental states on the quality of experience — before extending the frame across lives. The conflation of the two produces most of the popular confusion, including the reward-and-punishment picture neither tradition actually teaches.

Is “karma will get him” a legitimate use of the concept?

By the teaching’s own terms, no — and the errors stack: it externalizes a mechanism the teaching locates in consciousness, reduces a consequence-system to a punishment-system, assumes visible and timely manifestation the teaching never promises, and seats the speaker as spectator of someone else’s account — the one position the framework never assigns. The deeper cost is practical: attention spent tracking another person’s ledger is attention withdrawn from the only domain with your fingerprints on it. Research on just-world belief adds the diagnostic note: the “they’ll get theirs” reflex is a documented human default, which is precisely why the tradition redirects it.

What are the three types of karma?

In Hindu philosophy: sanchita, the accumulated stock of unripened karma across lifetimes; prarabdha, the portion allotted to the current lifetime — birth conditions, circumstances, tendencies — sometimes called fate but better read as starting conditions; and kriyamana (or agami), the karma being created through present action — the domain of active choice and the primary site of human agency. The structure answers the question that sinks the naive version: why do innocent people suffer? Starting conditions vary for reasons the current self can’t access; what happens within them is being created now.

Can someone clear or remove my karma for me?

No — and the offer itself is the tell. In every tradition that uses the word seriously, karma is worked with through practice and changed action: attention, intention, and what you do next. Nobody holds your account, which is exactly what makes the account-holder costume profitable — a reader who diagnoses your karmic debt and prices its removal has invented both the diagnosis and the billing. The honest version of “clearing” is the first-person work: examine what you’re creating, adjust the intention behind it, release the attachment to fruit. It costs nothing, and it’s the only mechanism the teaching contains.

Does karma mean innocent people deserve their suffering?

No — and the tripartite structure exists precisely to block that reading. Prarabdha karma (this life’s starting conditions) may reflect prior actions the current self has no conscious access to; that frames why conditions vary dramatically, and it does not make suffering acceptable or earned. Meanwhile those who cause harm are creating their own karma in real time — accountability isn’t erased, though its timeline isn’t ours to audit. The popular version’s “they must have deserved it” arithmetic is the just-world reflex, documented in psychology as a human default — and precisely the reading the teaching’s structure was built to prevent.

Can astrology or a psychic reading tell me about my karma?

Within the karma-attentive traditions, astrology reads the chart as a map of this lifetime’s conditions — South Node as accumulated material, Saturn as the organizing tests, Pluto as where transformation is required. Used honestly, that’s territory-description: the board, not the moves. A psychic reading framed on “what pattern is at work here and what is it asking of me” is squarely within what a thoughtful reading can engage. What neither can do is adjudicate anyone’s account, pronounce on deserts, or sell removal — those are marketplace inventions that the traditions themselves, on their own terms, disown.
